The only tree for four hundred kilometres of desert... and something still crashed into it. Twice.
In the Sahara, in north-east Niger, a single acacia stood alone in the sand. Nothing else grew around it, in any direction.
For about three hundred years, it was the landmark. Salt caravans of camels crossing between Agadez and Bilma steered by it. Lose it, and you were lost.
But how does one tree live where nothing else can?
In the winter of nineteen thirty-eight, a crew dug a well right beside it. Thirty-five metres down, they hit water. The tree's roots were already there, about as deep as a twelve-storey building is tall.
Then the cars came. Explorer Henri Lhote saw the tree in nineteen thirty-four, on the first car crossing between Djanet and Agadez. When he came back in nineteen fifty-nine, a vehicle had crashed into it.
And in nineteen seventy-three, a truck driver, reportedly drunk, drove straight into it and knocked it down.
Three hundred years of desert couldn't kill it. Two crashes did.
Today its remains rest in the National Museum of Niger. And where it stood, a metal tree keeps watch over the empty sand.
